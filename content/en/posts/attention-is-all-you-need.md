I just finished watching *Coyote vs. Acme*. Funny, with a lot of heart, and a few rough edges, sure. But I don't want to talk about movies right now.

What stuck with me was the undercurrent: the shots at big corporations and at the ambition to do whatever it takes in the name of "progress."

That reminded me of another loud story from the past few days. A researcher quit one of the frontier AI labs because he no longer agrees with how the whole industry seems willing to do anything to win the race. He said AI could end human life in under ten years, that the companies know it and still don't take the necessary measures. Colleagues from his own lab backed him up, admitting they don't have a plan to fix it either.

Watching that, and thinking about what we have already seen with other inventions capable of destroying humanity, I land on an uncomfortable thought: what can one person actually do from inside these structures? Quitting and warning the public out loud seems to be about all there is. Hans Jonas described that trap in *The Imperative of Responsibility* (1979): the consequences of our actions used to be local and temporary; today they can be global and permanent. His imperative was "act so that the effects of your action are compatible with the permanence of genuine human life." Put plainly: we build with enormous power and undo with empty hands. Once you handed the power over to something, you no longer have the power to take it back.

And still you wonder whether a warning is enough after you helped build the thing you fear.

Judgment always arrives late. David Collingridge named the problem in *The Social Control of Technology* (1980): you cannot control a technology when it starts, because you do not yet know what consequences it will bring; and by the time you do know, it has rooted itself too deep to stop. Which is why weapons, the atomic bomb or penicillin all end up filed as genius or brutality *afterward*, never at the moment of deciding. At the moment of deciding there was not enough information.

This individual dilemma reveals a deeper pattern that corporations seem to install without anyone noticing: you create the problem and then sell the solution to it, or warn about its possible causes. As long as nothing is known yet, or there is a funding round around the corner, anything goes in the name of "progress." Why? Because in the end what matters is being recognized.

There is an entire library behind that sentence. Hegel put the struggle for recognition (*Anerkennung*) at the engine of social life: we need others to value our agency. Rousseau had already seen the dark side of it in *amour-propre*, that need to compare ourselves and to be esteemed, which corrupts healthy self-love. Axel Honneth built a whole theory on it, and Pierre Bourdieu added the economic vocabulary it was missing: prestige is capital, symbolic capital, and it accumulates like any other. Each field decides which actions generate it. Robert Merton, for his part, documented the Matthew effect: whoever already has recognition gets more of it. The prestige system runs on cumulative advantage, so deliberate invisibility is not something anyone can choose and sustain.

No one is going to credit a company that, before launching a product, notices the potential harm and cancels the whole thing. Nor the engineer who quietly stopped a dangerous advance from the inside: at best nobody would know who it was, and at worst, better not to imagine it. The system as it is built rewards exactly two exits: creating "the future" or selling yourself as the one who came to "save" it. A warning that arrives in time, a silent cancellation, the prudent decision not to do something: none of that earns medals. And a structure that only recognizes those who cross the line, whether by building, destroying or rescuing, will keep producing, generation after generation, people who cross the line.

What I had trouble understanding was why people who are not bad keep going. Hannah Arendt covered Eichmann's trial in 1963 and came away with the phrase everyone misquotes: the banality of evil. The most destructive harm is almost never produced by a monster; it is produced by ordinary functionaries inside structures where the work is divided so finely that no one feels responsible for the whole. The juniors do not notice the danger; the seniors do, and they keep going anyway. Michael Walzer called the other half of this *dirty hands*: decent people make morally contaminated decisions because "someone is going to do it anyway." That is the exact argument the labs use to justify running the race, and the point where the pragmatic argument turns into an alibi without anyone signing for the change.

Maybe the question is not why the individuals inside these companies act the way they do, but why we built a world where those are the only actions that count.
